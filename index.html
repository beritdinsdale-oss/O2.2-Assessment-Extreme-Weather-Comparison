1 · Compare regions

Same summer. Different places.

Same year: 2021•Same season: Summer•Different: Region + event

Watch the events, or use the short summaries directly underneath.

Pacific Northwest · June 2021

Extreme heat

☀️
What happened?

A strong heat dome settled over the Pacific Northwest. Temperatures rose far above typical early-summer conditions and records were broken across the region.

Waverly, Tennessee · August 2021

Record rainfall

🌧️
What happened?

Exceptionally heavy rain fell around Waverly. More than 20 inches was recorded within 24 hours near the event area, producing extreme flash flooding.

Notice: The same season can bring very different extremes to different regions.
2 · Connect weather to climate

Why can warming influence different extremes?

Review the two short explanations, then complete one matching activity.

☀️ Extreme heat

Climate warms→Hot weather starts warmer→Extreme heat becomes more likely
Evidence: Strong

The link between human-caused warming and increasing extreme heat is well established.

🌧️ Heavy precipitation

Air warms→More water vapor can be available→Downpours can intensify
Evidence: Strong

Heavy precipitation has increased across much of the United States, although the amount of change differs by region.

Same region: Willamette Valley•Same year: 2021•Different: Season + event

Notice how different extremes can occur in different seasons.

What happened?

Freezing rain coated trees, roads, and power lines across parts of the Willamette Valley, producing damaging ice accumulation.

Explore the National Weather Service explanation (optional · new tab)
Climate evidence: Still evolving

Scientists understand how freezing rain forms, but are still studying how climate change may affect regional ice-storm frequency.

☀️

Summer 2021

Extreme heat

🌡️
What happened?

A few months after the ice storm, the same broader region experienced exceptional summer heat.

Climate evidence: Strong

Human-caused warming clearly increases the likelihood and intensity of extreme heat.

Seasonal pattern: A warming climate does not erase winter or summer. Different hazards can occur in different seasons, and the climate evidence is not equally strong for every hazard.
4 · Look across time

One event can’t show a climate trend

Long-term records reveal changes in how often extremes occur, how intense they become, and how long they last.

1960s→1980s→2000s→Today
☀️

Extreme heat

Heat waves have become hotter, more frequent, larger, and longer-lasting in recent decades.

View EPA heat visuals (new tab)
🌧️

Heavy precipitation

Heavy precipitation has been occurring more often across the contiguous United States since the 1950s, with regional differences.

View EPA precipitation visuals (new tab)
❄️

Cold extremes

Extreme cold events have generally become less frequent and less intense across much of the United States.

Long-term assessments evaluate each hazard separately.

This is the climate view: scientists look across decades, not just individual events.
5 · Put it together

What does the full pattern show?

📍Region

Different places can experience different extremes.

📅Season

Different extremes can occur at different times of year.

📈Time

Long-term records reveal whether extreme-weather patterns are changing.

Which statement best summarizes what you explored?

Key takeaway

An individual extreme event is weather. Patterns in the types, timing, location, frequency, and intensity of extreme weather over many years are part of climate.
